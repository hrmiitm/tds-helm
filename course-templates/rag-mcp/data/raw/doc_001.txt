Information Retrieval (IR) is the science of searching for information in a document, searching for documents themselves, and also searching for the metadata that describes data, or for databases of texts, images, or sounds. Automated information retrieval systems are used to reduce what has been called information overload. An IR system typically comprises several components: document indexing, query processing, matching and ranking, and evaluation.

Historically, traditional information retrieval has relied on sparse lexical representations. In sparse retrieval models, such as the classic Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) and BM25 (Best Matching 25), documents and queries are represented as high-dimensional, sparse vectors where each dimension corresponds to a specific word or token in the vocabulary. The weight of each term reflects its frequency within a document relative to its inverse frequency across the entire corpus. While BM25 remains an exceptionally strong baseline for exact keyword matching, technical jargon retrieval, and out-of-domain generalisation, it fundamentally suffers from vocabulary mismatch and term ambiguity problems. If a user queries "canine veterinarian care" and a document contains the terms "dog animal doctor services", sparse lexical matching fails to identify the semantic equivalence between the concepts unless explicit synonym expansion or query rewriting mechanisms are introduced.

To address these limitations, modern retrieval architectures have evolved towards semantic retrieval paradigms powered by dense vector representations. Rather than relying on exact token overlap, dense retrieval encodes passages and queries into continuous low-dimensional latent spaces where geometric proximity reflects conceptual similarity. The emergence of Transformer-based language models, such as BERT and its successors, revolutionized representation learning by capturing contextual relationships between words. Dual-encoder architectures, often called bi-encoders, map both queries and documents into a shared geometric embedding space using pre-trained neural networks.

In modern Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipelines, information retrieval serves as the critical first-stage filter. When an end-user poses a complex question to an AI system, relying solely on the parametric memory of a Large Language Model often leads to hallucinations, outdated assertions, or untraceable answers. By pairing the generative model with a deterministic retrieval mechanism, the system dynamically retrieves relevant document chunks from an external knowledge base. These retrieved chunks are synthesized and passed directly into the prompt context window of the generative model. This hybrid design ensures that responses are grounded in verified factual source material, enables verifiable citations, and facilitates cost-effective domain adaptation without the prohibitive compute requirements of full continuous model fine-tuning. Effective chunking strategies, embedding dimensionality, and index tuning are crucial factors that determine the overall precision and recall of the retrieval stage.
