In natural language processing and machine learning, an embedding is a dense, continuous vector representation of discrete objects such as words, sentences, passages, or entire documents. Unlike traditional one-hot encodings or sparse bag-of-words representations where vectors are orthogonal and proportional in size to the entire vocabulary, embeddings map textual data into dense mathematical spaces of fixed, lower dimensionality, typically ranging from 128 to 1536 dimensions. Within this latent space, geometric properties such as angle, Euclidean distance, and cosine similarity capture rich semantic and syntactic relationships.

The evolution of embedding techniques began with static word-level representations, notably Word2Vec and GloVe. These algorithms leveraged the distributional hypothesis—the linguistic principle that words occurring in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. By training shallow neural architectures to predict surrounding words from a target word or vice versa, Word2Vec learned dense vectors where relational analogies (such as vector arithmetic representing "king" - "man" + "woman" ≈ "queen") emerged naturally. However, static embeddings suffered from polysemy: a single vector was forced to represent words with multiple distinct meanings, such as "bank" referring to a financial institution or the side of a river.

The breakthrough of self-attention mechanisms and Transformer architectures solved polysemy through contextualized representation learning. Sentence-level and passage-level embedding models, such as Sentence-BERT (SBERT) and the all-MiniLM series, utilize Siamese and triplet network structures to fine-tune pre-trained Transformer backbones specifically for semantic similarity tasks. In standard BERT models, deriving a sentence embedding typically requires passing text through 12 to 24 layers and performing mean pooling over token embeddings. SentenceTransformers modify this objective during training using cosine-similarity loss, contrastive loss, or Multiple Negatives Ranking (MNR) loss, forcing semantically related sentences to cluster closely in Euclidean space.

For resource-constrained CPU environments, lightweight embedding models like all-MiniLM-L6-v2 provide an optimal Pareto frontier between inference latency and retrieval accuracy. Comprising only 6 Transformer layers with 384 embedding dimensions, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 processes text efficiently on standard multi-core CPUs without requiring dedicated GPU acceleration. When text is converted into these dense vectors, similarity between a user query vector and candidate chunk vectors is computed using cosine similarity or inner product. Normalized embeddings simplify this calculation to a standard dot product, enabling high-throughput vector comparison during downstream retrieval and indexing stages.
